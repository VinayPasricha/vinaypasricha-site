Summary

Vinay Pasricha points out that when the Roman Empire ended in 476, almost nobody noticed. There was no final battle worth the name; the end had been arriving for centuries. When experts produce so many explanations (the description cites more than 210), he says, they are describing symptoms. The simpler pattern underneath is that Rome didn't fall, it drifted: small misalignments compounding for generations. He says his book The Civilization asks whether drift can be detected in time, using eight directions of intelligence against which a nation can be read like an instrument panel. His line: strength is speed, but history grades direction, and collapse is rarely the event; it is the verdict.

Key points

  • Rome's end in 476 went almost unnoticed because it had been arriving for centuries.
  • The many explanations for Rome's fall describe symptoms, not the cause.
  • The underlying pattern is drift: small misalignments compounding over generations.
  • History grades direction, not just strength; collapse is the verdict, not the event.
