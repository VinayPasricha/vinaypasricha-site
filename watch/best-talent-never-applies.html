Summary

Vinay Pasricha argues that most companies don't have a hiring problem; they have a discovery problem. He describes how hiring usually works: write a job description, post it and hope the right person happens to see it, be looking and apply. "That's not a strategy, that's a lottery." You end up finding the best person who applied, not the best person for the role, and those are almost never the same. The people you actually need are already employed, good at what they do and too busy doing it well to search job boards, so the old model filters them out by design. It rewards availability over ability. He says he wrote Organizational Frequency to close this gap, because this is the age of intelligent discovery: companies no longer have to wait to be found and can match a role to the right person on actual fit, meaning what they have done, how they think and whether they genuinely thrive. But he stresses that technology is only half of it; the other half is doctrine, meaning how you think about talent and structure decisions, treating hiring as a discipline that compounds rather than a reactive scramble. A company's frequency is set by the people inside it: get them right and everything downstream gets easier; get them wrong and no strategy survives. The book, he says, is about getting people right on purpose, at scale.

Key points

  • Most companies have a discovery problem, not a hiring problem.
  • Post-and-wait hiring is a lottery that finds the best applicant, not the best person.
  • The right people are usually employed and not looking, so job postings filter them out and reward availability over ability.
  • Intelligent discovery lets companies match roles on actual fit, but it must be paired with a hiring doctrine.
  • A company's frequency is set by the people inside it.

That's not a strategy, that's a lottery.
