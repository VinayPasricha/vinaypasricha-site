Why Smart People Misread Reality — The SIV Method Explained

Summary

Vinay Pasricha argues that most failed decisions aren't failures of execution. They are "failures of understanding disguised as confidence." The SIV Method, which he describes as a brutal framework for understanding reality before acting on it, starts from an uncomfortable premise: the mind doesn't want truth, it wants resolution. To end the discomfort of not knowing, it closes the question early and offers a clean answer that feels like clarity but is really relief. He gives founder examples such as "our users churn because of price" and "this hire didn't work out because of attitude", and calls each one premature closure. Every plan built on such a distortion inherits the crack. His remedy is to stop trusting a single point of view, including your own. In the book this becomes a set of named lenses, deliberate angles of inquiry that bring up the dimensions you are blind to: not thinking harder, but thinking from more places. He warns against borrowed certainty, taking one confident perspective as the whole truth. The final principle is pressure before commitment: a claim earns the right to guide action only by surviving Socratic interrogation. He sums up the method in one line, and warns that shallow understanding doesn't feel shallow. It feels like speed and decisiveness, until months later you find you executed brilliantly on the wrong thing.

Key points

  • Most failed decisions are failures of understanding disguised as confidence, not failures of execution.
  • The mind seeks resolution, not truth, which leads to premature closure (for example, "users churn because of price").
  • Every plan built on a distortion inherits the crack.
  • Use multiple named lenses to think from more places instead of trusting one perspective. Borrowed certainty is how intelligent people walk off a cliff.
  • Pressure before commitment: a claim must survive Socratic interrogation before it guides action.

Before power is applied, reality must be examined hard enough to deserve action.
